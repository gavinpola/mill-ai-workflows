Mill Commercial Pricing Strategy

Hybrid base-fee-plus-platform-expansion pricing model for commercial food waste hardware. Validation: Whole Foods, Amazon Fresh, Google Cafeterias pilots (Q3-Q4 2027).

Executive Summary

Decision requested: Approve hybrid pricing model and $1.5M pilot commitment for Q3-Q4 2027. This memo provides the analysis supporting that decision.

Mill pays for itself in trash savings alone. Everything else is upside.

Strategic Thesis: Hybrid pricing ($500 base + 10% outcome share) delivers 6.1x LTV:CAC with 67% Year 1 margins, de-risked by hardware savings requiring no behavior change.

Recommendation

Adopt hybrid pricing: $500/month base fee + 10% outcome share, expanding to $825/month by Year 4 through modular value additions (base case at 40% attach rate; upside to $1,100/month at 70% attach).

Risk Reversal

90-day pilot exit clause: Customers may exit with zero penalty if documented trash savings are below $150/month during pilot period. Mill bears pilot risk. Full contractual details in Section 4.

If We're Wrong

Model survives individual metric failures; combined worst-case (P10 across all metrics simultaneously, <1% probability) requires cost restructure per Section 7. Alternative if board rejects hybrid model: Pure base-fee at $650/month (Model 1) preserves hardware economics but forgoes $3.5M Year 3 outcome share upside.

2. Pricing Model Analysis

Seven models, evaluated against Mill's specific context. Each with honest pros, cons, and a verdict.

Model 1: Pure Subscription Use as foundation
$500-800/month flat fee, no outcome component

Customer pays fixed amount regardless of results. Simple to explain, simple to budget. Mill captures value regardless of customer behavior.

Why it works
  • Predictable revenue for Mill
  • Trash savings justify base fee alone
  • Works for compliance customers
Why it's not enough
  • No proof of value to customer
  • Customer questions ROI after Year 1
  • No incentive to keep improving

Verdict: Use as foundation (base fee), but not sufficient alone for optimization value. Compliance-driven customers may accept this structure.

Model 2: Pure Outcome-Based Not viable
10-20% of documented savings, no base fee

Customer pays only for proven results. Zero risk to customer. Mill only earns when value is delivered.

Why it's appealing
  • Makes "yes" extremely easy
  • Aligns incentives perfectly
  • Forces Mill to prove value
Why it fails
  • Year 1 big, Year 2+ diminishes
  • Attribution fights ("Mill or new chef?")
  • Revenue unpredictable
  • Doesn't capture trash savings

Why Sierra can do this but Mill can't: AI agent resolution is unambiguous (ticket solved or not). Waste reduction attribution is messy (multiple factors affect food costs). Sierra's "outcome" is instantaneous; Mill's is cumulative over months.

Verdict: Not viable as primary model. Too much attribution complexity, revenue instability.

Model 3: Hybrid (Base + Outcome Share) Best foundation
$300-500/month base + 10-15% of documented savings

Base fee covers hardware and ongoing trash savings. Outcome share kicks in when food cost optimization delivers results. Customer gets "insurance" plus "upside."

Why it works
  • Base fee ensures revenue floor
  • Outcome share proves value
  • Base defensible via trash savings
  • Outcome share is upside, not core
Challenges to solve
  • Still need attribution methodology
  • Year 2+ outcome share will decline
  • Two components to explain

Verdict: Best foundation. Use as starting point and add mechanisms to address Year 2+ value (see Model 5) plus clear attribution rules (see Attribution Methodology section).

Model 4: Declining Outcome Share Over Time (In Isolation) Wrong in isolation
Year 1: 20%, Year 2: 15%, Year 3: 10%, Year 4+: 5% — with no compensating value

Explicitly recognizes that early wins are biggest. Customer sees price "decreasing" over time. Mill front-loads revenue. But this model includes no new value to offset the decline.

Why it seems appealing
  • Matches reality (big wins early)
  • Customer likes "cheaper over time"
  • Predictable decline vs. unpredictable
Why it's wrong IN ISOLATION
  • Total revenue declines over time
  • Signals declining partnership value
  • No new value justifies continued relationship
  • Customer asks "why am I still paying?"

Verdict: Reject as standalone model. However, declining outcome share WORKS when paired with expanding value (modules, benchmarks) that more than offsets the decline. See Model 5 for the correct implementation: outcome share declines from 10% to 5%, but total revenue INCREASES because new modules add $200-475/month in value.

Model 5: Escalating Value + Price (Platform Expansion) Recommended
Base fee + modules that unlock over time

Customer starts with core value. New value unlocks as data accumulates. Each new module is a natural upsell. Price increases with value delivered.

The expansion model
Year 1: $500/month (waste tracking + trash savings)
Year 2: +$200/month (food cost module - unlocks after 12 months data)
Year 3: +$150/month (benchmark access - meaningful at network scale)
Year 4: +$100/month (enterprise features)
Revenue increases despite outcome share declining
Why it works
  • Solves the decline problem
  • Matches data value curve
  • Natural expansion, not extraction
  • Retention through value
Requirements
  • Must build the modules (see Product Roadmap)
  • Must prove each module's ROI
  • Year 1 must be compelling enough to start

Verdict: This is the strategic direction. Requires product investment but solves multi-year problem. Customer isn't "paying more for same thing"—they're getting new things.

Model 6: Enterprise Data Subscription Use for enterprise
Annual commitment + 3-5% escalator + inflation

Customer commits to annual contract with built-in escalator. Positions Mill as "data infrastructure" not "optimization tool."

The framing shift: NOT "We help you save on food costs." YES "We provide continuous access to operational intelligence."

Verdict: Use for enterprise customers. Pairs well with platform expansion model. Procurement teams understand annual contracts with escalators.

Model 7: Guaranteed Savings (Risk Reversal) Use selectively
"We guarantee X% reduction OR you don't pay"

Mill takes on all the risk. Customer has zero downside to try. Confidence signal that Mill believes in the product.

Why it's appealing
  • Makes "yes" trivially easy
  • Cuts through objections
  • Shows confidence
Why it's dangerous
  • Adverse selection risk
  • Customer could game baseline
  • No base for hardware cost
  • Margin pressure

Safe structure: "We guarantee 15% trash cost reduction in Year 1 OR we reduce your Year 2 base fee by the shortfall." Risk reversal + tied to renewal + based on trash (not food waste attribution).

Verdict: Use selectively for high-value prospects who need confidence signal. Not primary model. Cap the credit at 50% of Year 2 base fee.

Implication for pricing: Hybrid model (Model 3) captures floor via base fee while preserving upside via outcome share. Pure subscription (Model 1) or pure outcome-based (Model 2) each sacrifice either downside protection or growth potential. Model 3 combined with platform expansion (Model 5) satisfies both board risk tolerance and customer ROI expectations.

3. Attribution Methodology

Outcome-based pricing fails when attribution is vague. This section defines exactly how savings are calculated, what's included, what's excluded, and how disputes are resolved.

The Attribution Problem

When a customer's food waste drops 20%, multiple factors could be responsible: Mill's insights, a new chef, supplier change, menu redesign, seasonal shift, economic pressure. Pure outcome pricing on "total waste reduction" creates endless disputes.

Mill's Attribution Framework

1. Separate Measurable from Behavioral

Category Mill Attribution Why
Trash volume reduction 100% Mill-attributed Physical dehydration is mechanical, not behavioral. Mill hardware causes this directly.
Food cost reduction (behavior-driven) Shared attribution via outcome share Customer acts on insights. Mill provides intelligence, customer provides action.

Key insight: The base fee is justified by trash savings alone (Mill-attributed). The outcome share is for the behavior-driven food cost reduction (shared attribution).

2. Baseline Methodology

Baseline establishment process
Days 1-90: Baseline capture period
  • Mill tracks waste patterns without intervention
  • No insights delivered to customer during baseline
  • Customer operates normally

Day 91+: Optimization begins
  • Insights delivered
  • Savings calculated against 90-day baseline

Rolling baseline: Every 12 months, baseline recalculates
  • Prevents permanent baseline gaming
  • Accounts for business evolution
90-day baseline + 12-month rolling reset

3. Material Change Protocol

What happens when the customer's business changes significantly?

Event Impact on Attribution
New executive chef hired 30-day baseline pause. New baseline established. Prior savings locked in.
Menu overhaul (>30% items changed) 60-day baseline recapture for affected categories.
Supplier change for major ingredient That ingredient category excluded from outcome share for 60 days.
Location expansion/contraction Per-location baselines. Portfolio changes don't reset existing locations.
Seasonal business (>40% volume shift) Seasonal baselines (Q1-Q2 vs Q3-Q4). Compare to same period prior year.

4. Anti-Gaming Provisions

Gaming Vector 1: Baseline Inflation
Customer artificially wastes more during baseline period to inflate future "savings."

Mitigation: Mill compares baseline to industry benchmarks. If baseline is >2 standard deviations above category average, Mill flags for review. Contract includes "reasonable operations" clause.

Gaming Vector 2: System Circumvention
Customer disposes waste outside Mill system to avoid logging.

Mitigation: Outcome share requires minimum 80% waste capture rate (estimated via food purchase volume correlation). Below 80% = outcome share suspended.

Gaming Vector 3: Claiming External Credit
"The savings came from our new chef, not Mill."

Mitigation: Material change protocol (see above). Chef change triggers baseline reset. Prior savings already locked in and paid.

5. Dispute Resolution

Step Timeline Process
Customer dispute filed Within 14 days of invoice Written dispute with specific calculation objection
Mill review 14 days Data review, methodology check, response with findings
Escalation (if unresolved) 30 days Third-party auditor review (cost split 50/50)
Final decision Binding Auditor decision is final. Credit or payment within 30 days.

Outcome Share: Worked Example

Monthly outcome share calculation — typical restaurant
Formula:
Monthly Outcome Share = (Baseline Waste Cost - Current Waste Cost) × Outcome Share % × Attribution Factor

Baseline (90-day average):
Food waste: 150 lbs/day @ average $2.50/lb = $375/day = $11,250/month

Post-optimization (Day 91+):
Food waste: 120 lbs/day @ $2.50/lb = $300/day = $9,000/month

Monthly savings:
$11,250 - $9,000 = $2,250/month food waste reduction

Mill outcome share (10%):
$2,250 × 10% × 1.0 (no material changes) = $225/month

Range expectations across customer base:
  • Low performer (10% waste reduction): ~$100-125/month outcome share
  • Average performer (20% reduction): ~$200-250/month
  • High performer (30% reduction): ~$300-375/month
Model estimate of ~$150/month is conservative/median assumption

10% outcome share rationale: HVAC energy savings contracts typically share 15-25%; utility rebate programs charge 10-15% administrative fee; waste-to-energy partnerships share 10-20%. Mill uses 10% (lower end) because food waste attribution is more complex than energy savings.

Why This Works

For Mill: Clear methodology reduces support burden. Disputes have defined process. Anti-gaming provisions protect revenue integrity.

For Customer: Transparent calculation. Known dispute process. Material change protection. No "gotcha" attribution fights.

The key principle: Base fee is for mechanical value (trash reduction). Outcome share is for behavioral value (food cost optimization). Never mix attribution between the two.

Implication for pricing: Attribution methodology enables hybrid model defensibility. Clear separation of mechanical vs. behavioral value prevents customer disputes and supports outcome-share pricing without operational friction.

Mill's Differentiation: Elimination vs. Tracking

All competitors help you TRACK waste. They tell you what you wasted. You still pay to haul it away. Even hardware-based competitors (Winnow Vision, Kitro, Orbisk) provide measurement, not reduction.

Mill helps you ELIMINATE waste. Our hardware dehydrates waste, reducing volume 60-80%. Your hauling costs drop immediately, regardless of whether you act on insights.

Net cost differential: Competitors charge $500-1,000/month with no hauling reduction. Mill charges $500/month while reducing hauling costs $200-400/month. Result: Mill's effective cost is 40-50% lower than sticker-price comparison suggests.

7. Risk Analysis

What can go wrong, and what's the plan?

What We Cannot Validate Pre-Pilot:

  1. Commercial CV accuracy at scale — Consumer pilot data may not transfer to commercial kitchen complexity. Validation event: Whole Foods Month 6.
  2. Enterprise procurement cycle length — Assumed 3-4 months based on comparables; actual may differ by 50%+. Validation event: First 10 enterprise contracts.
  3. Module attach rate — 40% base case derived from SaaS benchmarks; no Mill-specific data until Month 18. Model viable at 0% attach.

These unknowns are explicitly tracked in Kill Criteria below. Pricing pivot paths exist for each adverse scenario.

Hardware Failure Medium

Risk: Unit fails during peak service, disrupting operations and data continuity.

Mitigation: 4-hour response SLA. Same-day replacement shipping. Enterprise accounts get on-site loaner inventory. Outcome share excludes downtime days.

Residual risk: Multi-day outages possible in remote locations. Acceptable given overall reliability targets.

Module Adoption Failure High

Risk: Customers don't buy Year 2+ modules, revenue trajectory fails.

Mitigation: Base fee + trash savings model is profitable even at zero module attach (see Unit Economics). Modules are upside, not survival.

Contingency: If attach rates below 30%, shift to bundled pricing (include modules in base at lower total price) to capture value through retention rather than upsell.

Competitive Response Medium

Risk: Winnow or Leanpath adds dehydration hardware, eliminating differentiation.

Mitigation: Mill's consumer unit is 3+ years ahead in development. Commercial hardware builds on this. Patent protection on key mechanisms. Network effects grow with scale.

Contingency: If competitors match hardware, shift differentiation to data network (benchmarks, procurement intelligence) which takes 3+ years to replicate.

Economic Downturn Medium

Risk: Restaurants cut "optimization tools" first in recession.

Mitigation: Mill's base value (trash savings) is cost reduction, not cost addition. Counter-cyclical pitch: "Cut costs now."

Contingency: Offer flexible terms (month-to-month at premium, or annual at discount) to retain price-sensitive customers.

Attribution Disputes Low (with mitigation)

Risk: Customers dispute outcome share calculations, creating support burden and churn.

Mitigation: Clear attribution methodology (see Attribution section). Third-party auditor for escalated disputes. Material change protocol.

Residual risk: Some disputes inevitable. Budget for ~5% of outcome share revenue in dispute resolution costs.

Regulatory Change Low

Risk: SB 1383 weakened or repealed, reducing compliance-driven demand.

Mitigation: Compliance is ~20% of value proposition. Cost savings (trash, food cost) drive majority of demand. ESG requirements (B-Corp, investor pressure) provide regulatory backstop.
