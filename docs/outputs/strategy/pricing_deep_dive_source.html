Value Strategy: First Principles

How Mill maximizes customer lifetime value without diluting its own position. An honest assessment of what creates retention, where the money is, and how to price for it.

Waste as Entry Point

Commercial kitchens throw away 4-10% of purchased food. That's measurable, painful, and hard to track accurately. Every competitor in this space relies on estimates or self-reported data. Mill verifies waste through physical processing—if it went through the unit, it was actually wasted.

This is the credible foundation: verified waste data that no one else has.

But waste is a small slice of what kitchens spend. The strategic question is whether Mill stays a waste tool or becomes something larger.

Where Kitchen Money Actually Goes

Category % of Revenue Annual $ (for $2M location)
Food costs 28-35% $560K-700K
Labor 28-35% $560K-700K
Rent/occupancy 5-10% $100K-200K
Equipment/technology 3-5% $60K-100K

Industry estimates put waste at 4-10% of food costs; 7% is a reasonable midpoint. For a $2M restaurant, that's ~$42K/year wasted. If Mill helps reduce waste by half and takes 15% of savings, that's $3,150/year—about $262/month.

This is the ceiling if waste is the only value. To capture more, Mill must expand into food cost optimization, labor efficiency, or procurement intelligence—larger buckets where the same data creates additional value.

What Actually Creates Retention

The uncomfortable scenario: a customer uses Mill for six months, learns where their waste is, fixes the problem, and cancels. They extracted the insight; Mill loses the recurring revenue.

Not everything that sounds like a moat actually prevents this.

Mechanisms That Work

Mechanism Why It Creates Retention
Physical hardware The unit is installed. Removing it requires labor and disruption. Not exciting, but real.
Compounding predictions Early predictions rely on category-level patterns. Over time, location-specific signals (seasonal, staffing, events) improve accuracy. The hypothesis: leaving after 12 months means losing that learned context.
Integration depth If Mill connects to POS, ordering systems, and prep software, leaving means rebuilding all those connections.

Mechanisms That Don't (Yet)

Mechanism Why It Falls Short
"Insights" Once you know chicken waste spikes on Fridays, you know. You don't need Mill to remember.
"Benchmarking" at small scale 50 kitchens isn't a meaningful peer set. "You're in the 60th percentile of 50 restaurants" doesn't drive decisions.
"Continuous monitoring" If they can hire someone to weigh trash for $15/hour and Mill costs $500/month, the math has to work.

The honest assessment: Hardware creates real switching costs. Compounding predictions require building models that actually get meaningfully better with more data. Industry benchmarking requires scale Mill doesn't have yet—it's a future moat, not a current one.

Three Paths Forward

Where Mill ends up depends on product decisions made now. Each path has different economics.

The first scenario is where Mill starts. The question is whether product and go-to-market are designed to evolve toward the second or third.

What Expansion Looks Like

Starting Point Expansion Additional Lock-In
Waste tracking Food cost management Financial data integration
Food cost management Menu optimization Menu planning workflows
Menu optimization Labor planning Scheduling systems
Labor planning Supplier negotiation Procurement relationships

Each expansion creates switching costs. Toast, the restaurant tech platform, built this playbook: start with POS, expand into payroll, scheduling, and marketing. Their net revenue retention exceeds 115% because customers keep adding modules. Mill can follow the same expansion pattern.

Pricing Structure

Component Amount Purpose
Base fee $500-800/month Covers hardware amortization, monitoring, and benchmark access. Hardware economics TBD based on unit costs.
Outcome share 10-15% of documented savings Proves value. Mill only earns upside when the system performs.
Expansion pricing Per-location, volume discounts Captures more value as customer grows. Built into initial contract.

Why Hybrid

Alternative Problem
Pure outcome-based Revenue unpredictable. Creates attribution arguments. ("Was that savings from Mill or from our new chef?")
Pure subscription No proof of value. Customer questions ROI every renewal.
Hybrid Base fee covers costs. Outcome share proves value. Both sides win when system performs.

Sierra makes pure outcome-based work because their value is unambiguous: a customer service conversation either got resolved without human escalation or it didn't. Binary. Timestamped. Waste reduction is messier—was the 20% reduction from Mill's insights or the new chef? A hybrid model sidesteps this attribution problem while still aligning incentives.

The Data Revenue Layer

Separate from what customers pay, there's a potential revenue stream from aggregated data.

What Mill uniquely has: item-level waste data verified by physical processing, connected to actual behavior. Not surveys. Not estimates.

Who pays for this: CPGs spend $500K-2M per research study trying to understand what happens to their products after purchase. "Your product is wasted 40% more than competitors in the same category. Here's when and why." That's valuable intelligence they can't get elsewhere.

Directional estimate (assumptions will need validation)
500 kitchens × $1K/year data fee = $500K/year (customer side)
CPG contracts: Highly dependent on data depth, exclusivity, and coverage. Range could be $200K-1M per contract.
Total data revenue potential: $1-3M/year at scale

Constraints: This requires 500+ kitchens for statistically meaningful data, privacy/aggregation infrastructure, and CPGs who see value before committing. It's a Year 3+ opportunity, not Year 1.

Commercial Formula

The go-to-market needs a promise that's simple to make and easy to verify.

"We reduce food waste by 15-25%. That's $X/year for your operation. You pay a base fee for the hardware and system. If we hit targets, we share in the savings. As you add locations, we grow together."

Why This Works

  • Promise is specific: 15-25% reduction, not "significant improvement"
  • Risk is limited: Base fee covers costs, outcome share is pure upside
  • Category is validated: Winnow claims 40-70% waste reduction for their customers. Leanpath (acquired by IDEO) claims 50%+ reductions. 15-25% is conservative.
  • Expansion is built in: Multi-location terms in initial contract, no renegotiation

Pre-Product Credibility

Mill doesn't have hardware in market until 2027. Credibility before that comes from:

  • Technical demonstration: Show CV classification accuracy on test data
  • Design partner pilots: Even unpaid, having recognizable brands testing creates signal
  • Category validation: Winnow raised $45M, Leanpath was acquired—the market exists

The Strategic Frame

How Mill talks about itself shapes what customers expect and what the company can become.

Framing Problem
"We're a food operations platform" Aspirational. Not credible until the product proves it.
"We're a waste tracking tool" Undervalues the opportunity. Invites comparison to commodity competitors.

A possible frame: "We're building the infrastructure layer for food operations. We start with waste because it's the most acute pain and the hardest data to get accurately. The same system that classifies waste can optimize food costs, influence procurement, and benchmark operations against industry peers."

This framing is credible now (waste tracking is real), expansive over time (platform story), simple to sell (15-25% reduction), and positions for maximum long-term value.

Summary

Entry point:

Waste reduction is the credible, painful, data-rich starting point. But it's a small slice of kitchen economics—roughly $250-500/month ceiling per location.

Expansion path:

The same data that classifies waste can optimize food costs, labor, and procurement. Each expansion creates switching costs and captures more value.

Retention mechanism:

Hardware lock-in is real. Compounding predictions require models that actually improve with more data. Industry benchmarking requires scale—a future moat, not a current one.

Pricing:

Hybrid model. Base fee ($500-800/month) covers costs and creates predictability. Outcome share (10-15% of savings) proves value and aligns incentives.

Data revenue:

At scale, aggregated waste data becomes valuable to CPGs. $2-3M/year potential. Year 3+ opportunity, not Year 1.
